Class Notes: Communication

📌 Core Definitions

- Message: Raw data which is sent by the sender for the receiver.
- Information: Organized data, or meaningful data.
- Sender: Initiator, encoder, transmitter.
- Source: Originator.

📡 Bandwidth Requirements

Standard bandwidth ranges for different types of signals:

- Speech: 300 Hz to 3400 Hz
- Music: Up to 20 kHz
- Video: 4.2 MHz
- TV: 6 MHz

🔊 Noise[^1] Classification

Noise is categorized into two main types:

#### 1. Internal Noise:[^2]

- Thermal noise[^3]
- Shot noise[^4]
- Flicker noise[^5]

####  2. External Noise:[^6]

- Atmospheric noise[^7]
- Cosmic noise[^8]
- Manmade noise[^9]

##### 🧮 Signal to Noise Ratio[^10]  & Noise Factor , Noise Figure[^12] .

- Signal to noise ratio: Measured at a certain point.
- Noise Factor[^11] Calculation:
  $$F = \frac{\text{Input SNR}}{\text{Output SNR}}$$

[^1]: **Definition of Noise:** In communication systems, **noise** is defined as an unwanted electrical signal that interferes with the transmission, reception, and processing of the desired information signal.
[^2]: Internal noise is generated by the active and passive electrical components (such as resistors, diodes, and transistors) within the communication circuit itself.
[^3]: **Thermal Noise (Johnson-Nyquist Noise)**
	
	- **Cause:** It is caused by the random, thermally induced motion of free electrons inside a conductor.
	    
	- **Characteristics:** The intensity of this noise is directly proportional to the absolute temperature. It is present in all passive resistive components and is uniformly distributed across the frequency spectrum (White Noise).
	
	### mathemeticaly 
	
	$P_n = kT   \delta f$ 
	where 
	$P_n$ is the noise power 
	k is boltz man constant $1.38 \times 10 ^ 23$
	T is absolute temprature 
	$\delta f$ is the bandwidth . 
	
	
	
[^4]: **B. Shot Noise**
	
	- **Cause:** It arises due to the discrete (particle-like) nature of charge carriers (electrons or holes) moving across a junction or potential barrier (e.g., in a P-N junction diode or transistor).
	    
	- **Characteristics:** Instead of flowing continuously, electrons arrive at a destination randomly, creating fluctuations in the current. It is compared to the sound of lead shots falling on a metal sheet.
[^5]: **C. Flicker Noise (1/f Noise)**
	
	- **Cause:** It is primarily caused by structural defects, impurities, and surface imperfections in semiconductor materials.
	    
	- **Characteristics:** It is highly significant at lower frequencies and its power decreases inversely as the frequency increases (hence the name 1/f noise).
[^6]: External noise originates from sources outside the communication receiver or circuit. Its effects can be minimized by changing the location of the antenna or shifting to a different frequency band.
[^7]: **A. Atmospheric Noise (Static Noise)**
	
	- **Cause:** It is produced by natural electrical disturbances occurring in the Earth's atmosphere, most notably lightning discharges and thunderstorms.
	    
	- **Characteristics:** It travels in the form of electromagnetic waves and severely affects low and medium-frequency radio bands (AM radio). Its intensity is higher during rainy or stormy weather.
[^8]: **B. Cosmic Noise (Extraterrestrial Noise)**
	
	- **Cause:** It originates from sources outside the Earth's atmosphere. It is divided into two sub-categories:
	    
	    - _Solar Noise:_ Electromagnetic radiation continuously emitted by the Sun.
	        
	    - _Galactic Noise:_ Radiation from distant stars, galaxies, and other celestial bodies.
	        
	- **Characteristics:** It mostly affects high-frequency receivers, especially those pointed towards the sky (like satellite communication antennas).
[^9]: **C. Man-made Noise (Industrial Noise)**
	
	- **Cause:** It is generated by human-made electrical and electromechanical devices.
	    
	- **Sources:** Automobile ignition systems, electric motors, high-voltage power lines, heavy machinery, and fluorescent lighting.
	    
	- **Characteristics:** It is highly prominent in densely populated urban and industrial areas.
[^10]: **Definition:** Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is the ratio of the desired signal power to the unwanted noise power at a specific point in a communication circuit. 
	
	**Mathematical Expression:**
	
	- **Linear Ratio:** SNR = $P_{signal} / P_{noise}$_(where $P_{signal}$ is the signal power and $P_{noise}$ is the noise power)_
	    
	- **In Decibels (dB):** SNR (dB) = $10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_{signal}}{P_{noise}} \right)$
	    
	
	**Key Characteristics:**
	
	- **Significance:** It determines the quality of a communication system. A higher SNR indicates a clearer, stronger signal with less background noise.
	    
	- **Measurement:** It is always measured at a specific single point in the system (e.g., at the input of a receiver, or at the output of an amplifier).
[^11]: **A. Noise Factor (F):****Definition:** It is the ratio of the Signal-to-Noise ratio at the input of a device to the Signal-to-Noise ratio at its output.
	
	**Formula:**
	
	$$F = \frac{\text{Input SNR}}{\text{Output SNR}}$$
	
	**Key Characteristics of Noise Factor (F):**
	
	- Because every electronic device adds its own internal noise (like thermal or shot noise), the Output SNR is always lower than the Input SNR.
	    
	- Therefore, the Noise Factor (F) of a practical system is always **greater than 1**.
	    
	- For an _ideal_, noiseless device, F = 1.
[^12]: **Noise Figure (NF):** 
	
	**Definition:** The Noise Figure is simply the Noise Factor (F) expressed in decibels (dB). Exam questions often use "Noise Figure" and "Noise Factor" interchangeably, but technically, NF is the logarithmic version of F.
	
	**Formula:**
	
	$$\text{NF (in dB)} = 10 \log_{10} (F)$$
	
	**Why it matters:** In system design, a lower Noise Figure means the device is of higher quality because it introduces very little additional noise to the signal passing through it.
